01 · Overview

Apps keep changing. Our thumbs don't get the memo.

Question

What is the cognitive load users bear due to subtle changes in frequently used interfaces?

Approach

A comparative experiment: people complete 5 everyday tasks on an existing and a changed interface, then rate their workload with NASA-TLX.

Status

Study designed and presented as an IST 520 final proposal. Data collection is the next step.

02 · The problem

Habit is a feature. Redesigns quietly break it.

01 · Habit

We spend hours a day in the same apps.

That repetition turns into habitual, automatic behaviour.

02 · Muscle memory

Repeated actions become unconscious.

We stop looking for the button; our thumb just goes there.

03 · Disruption

A small move breaks the loop.

Even minor UI shifts force users back into conscious, effortful thinking, and a new learning curve.

04 · Trade-off

Worth it to the company. And to users?

Platforms redesign to drive engagement. The cost to users is rarely measured.

04 · Research gap

What the literature hasn't measured yet.

  1. Very few studies measure how real UI changes disrupt muscle memory and, in turn, cognitive load.
  2. Existing studies rarely test UI changes with real-world tasks that reflect actual app use.
  3. The link between habitual digital behaviour and redesign acceptance remains unclear.

05 · Method

Five everyday tasks, two versions of the same app.

A comparative survey experiment. Participants complete the same five structured tasks on the existing interface and on a changed one. I record time on task for each, to find performance differences caused by the change.

T1Post a photo
T2Send a direct message
T3Access call logs
T4Change notification settings
T5Change password

Tasks range from daily, deeply habitual actions (posting, messaging) to rare, settings-level ones, so habit strength varies across the set.

07 · Why it matters

Responsible redesign.

Designers have to balance innovation with user habit. Measuring the gap between the two helps teams plan smoother redesigns: staged rollouts, preserving high-frequency targets, and onboarding for changed paths.

Next steps: I built the study kit: a working mock app in a familiar “before” layout and a redesigned “after” layout, with automatic timing and NASA-TLX after every task. Next: recruit participants, run sessions and compare workload and task time across conditions.
